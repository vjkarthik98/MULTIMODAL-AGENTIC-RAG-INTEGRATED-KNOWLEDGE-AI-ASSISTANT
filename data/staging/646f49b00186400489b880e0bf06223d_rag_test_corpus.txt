[DOC-001] Introduction to Transformer Architecture
Transformers are a type of neural network architecture introduced in the 2017
paper "Attention Is All You Need" by Vaswani et al. Unlike recurrent neural
networks (RNNs), transformers process input sequences in parallel using a
mechanism called self-attention. Self-attention allows each token in the input
to attend to all other tokens, capturing long-range dependencies efficiently.
A transformer consists of an encoder and a decoder, each made up of stacked
layers. Each layer contains a multi-head self-attention sub-layer and a
position-wise feed-forward network. Layer normalization and residual connections
are applied around each sub-layer. Positional encodings are added to input
embeddings to inject sequential order information, since transformers have no
inherent notion of position.

[DOC-002] Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is a technique that enhances large language
model (LLM) responses by retrieving relevant documents from an external knowledge
base before generating an answer. The pipeline has two main stages: retrieval
and generation. In the retrieval stage, a query is embedded into a vector space
and compared against pre-indexed document embeddings using similarity search
(often cosine similarity or dot product). The top-k most relevant chunks are
retrieved. In the generation stage, these chunks are appended to the prompt as
context, and the LLM generates a response grounded in that context.
RAG reduces hallucinations, allows knowledge to be updated without retraining,
and enables citation of sources. Common retrieval backends include FAISS,
Pinecone, Weaviate, Qdrant, and Chroma.

[DOC-003] Vector Embeddings Explained
A vector embedding is a dense numerical representation of data (text, images,
audio) in a high-dimensional space. Semantically similar items are mapped to
nearby points in this space. For text, embedding models like OpenAI's
text-embedding-ada-002, Sentence-BERT, or BGE produce embeddings of fixed
dimensionality (e.g., 768 or 1536 dimensions). Embeddings are generated by
passing input through a trained encoder and extracting activations from a
specific layer. Approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN) search algorithms like HNSW
(Hierarchical Navigable Small World) and IVF (Inverted File Index) enable fast
retrieval over millions of vectors. Embedding quality is measured by benchmarks
such as MTEB (Massive Text Embedding Benchmark).

[DOC-004] Chunking Strategies in RAG
Chunking refers to splitting documents into smaller pieces before embedding and
indexing. Fixed-size chunking splits text every N tokens (e.g., 256 or 512),
with optional overlap (e.g., 50 tokens) to preserve context across boundaries.
Semantic chunking splits text at natural boundaries like paragraphs or sentences.
Hierarchical chunking creates parent and child chunks, retrieving children but
passing parents as context. The choice of chunk size involves a trade-off:
smaller chunks improve retrieval precision but reduce context richness; larger
chunks carry more context but may dilute relevance. Overlapping chunks help
prevent information loss at boundaries.

[DOC-005] Evaluation Metrics for RAG Systems
Key metrics for evaluating RAG pipelines include:
- Faithfulness: Does the generated answer stay true to the retrieved context?
- Answer Relevancy: Is the answer relevant to the original question?
- Context Precision: How much of the retrieved context is actually useful?
- Context Recall: Was all necessary information retrieved?
- RAGAS Score: A composite metric combining faithfulness, answer relevancy,
  context precision, and context recall.
Hallucination rate measures how often the model introduces facts not in context.
End-to-end latency and retrieval latency are operational metrics. Human
evaluation and LLM-as-judge approaches are also widely used for nuanced quality
assessment.

[DOC-006] The Carbon Cycle
Carbon cycles through the Earth system via photosynthesis, respiration,
decomposition, ocean absorption, and volcanic outgassing. Plants absorb CO₂
during photosynthesis, converting it to organic matter. When organisms respire
or decompose, carbon is released back to the atmosphere. Oceans absorb roughly
25–30% of anthropogenic CO₂ emissions, acting as a major carbon sink. However,
increased ocean CO₂ leads to acidification, threatening marine ecosystems.
Permafrost in the Arctic stores vast amounts of organic carbon; as it thaws due
to rising temperatures, it releases CH₄ (methane) and CO₂, creating a positive
feedback loop that amplifies warming.

[DOC-007] Renewable Energy Sources
Solar photovoltaic (PV) panels convert sunlight directly into electricity using
the photoelectric effect in semiconductor materials like silicon. As of 2024,
the global average cost of utility-scale solar PV has fallen below $0.05/kWh,
making it the cheapest source of electricity in history. Wind turbines harness
kinetic energy from wind to rotate blades connected to a generator. Offshore
wind typically has higher capacity factors (40–50%) than onshore wind (25–35%)
due to stronger, more consistent winds. Hydropower remains the largest source
of renewable electricity globally, contributing about 16% of total electricity
generation. Geothermal energy taps heat from the Earth's interior and is
particularly viable in tectonically active regions like Iceland and Kenya.

[DOC-008] Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services
Biodiversity refers to the variety of life at genetic, species, and ecosystem
levels. Ecosystem services are the benefits humans derive from nature, grouped
into provisioning (food, water, timber), regulating (climate regulation,
pollination, flood control), cultural (recreation, spiritual value), and
supporting services (nutrient cycling, soil formation). The Intergovernmental
Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services (IPBES) estimates
that approximately one million species face extinction due to habitat loss,
overexploitation, pollution, invasive species, and climate change. Protected
areas cover about 17% of terrestrial land and 10% of marine areas globally,
though coverage and enforcement vary widely.

[DOC-009] The Human Immune System
The immune system defends the body against pathogens through two main branches:
innate immunity and adaptive immunity. Innate immunity provides immediate,
non-specific defense using physical barriers (skin, mucus), phagocytes
(neutrophils, macrophages), and the complement system. Adaptive immunity mounts
a targeted response via T lymphocytes and B lymphocytes. B cells produce
antibodies—proteins that bind specifically to antigens on pathogens and neutralize
them. T cells include cytotoxic T cells (which kill infected cells) and helper
T cells (which coordinate the immune response). Immunological memory enables
faster, stronger responses upon re-exposure to a pathogen, which is the
biological basis of vaccination.

[DOC-010] mRNA Vaccine Technology
Messenger RNA (mRNA) vaccines work by delivering genetic instructions that
encode a target antigen (e.g., the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein) into host cells.
Ribosomes translate the mRNA into antigen protein, which triggers an immune
response. The mRNA degrades within days and never enters the cell nucleus, so
it cannot alter DNA. Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) are used to encapsulate and
deliver mRNA, protecting it from degradation. Advantages of mRNA vaccines
include rapid development (weeks from sequence to candidate), scalable
manufacturing, and platform flexibility. The Pfizer-BioNTech and Moderna
COVID-19 vaccines demonstrated 90–95% efficacy in clinical trials, accelerating
global interest in mRNA for cancer vaccines and other therapeutic applications.

[DOC-011] CRISPR-Cas9 Gene Editing
CRISPR-Cas9 (Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats) is a
molecular tool for precise genome editing. A guide RNA (gRNA) directs the Cas9
endonuclease to a specific DNA sequence, where it makes a double-strand break.
The cell repairs the break via non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), which often
introduces insertions or deletions (indels) that disrupt gene function, or via
homology-directed repair (HDR) using a provided template to introduce precise
edits. Applications include treating genetic diseases (e.g., sickle cell
disease), agricultural improvements, and basic research. Challenges include
off-target edits, delivery to specific tissues, and ethical concerns around
germline editing. In 2023, the FDA approved the first CRISPR-based therapy,
Casgevy, for sickle cell disease and beta-thalassemia.

SECTION 4: WORLD HISTORY & GEOPOLITICS

[DOC-012] The Industrial Revolution
The Industrial Revolution began in Britain around 1760 and spread to Europe and
North America by the mid-19th century. Key drivers included the steam engine
(perfected by James Watt in 1769), mechanized textile production (spinning jenny,
power loom), and iron/steel manufacturing. Coal replaced wood as the primary
energy source, fueling factories and railways. Urbanization accelerated as
workers migrated from farms to cities. The period saw dramatic improvements in
living standards and productivity alongside severe social challenges: child
labor, poor working conditions, and environmental pollution. The Industrial
Revolution laid the foundation for modern capitalism and fundamentally
transformed global economic and social structures.

[DOC-013] The Cold War (1947–1991)
The Cold War was a geopolitical competition between the United States and the
Soviet Union following World War II. It was characterized by ideological rivalry
(capitalism vs. communism), arms races (nuclear weapons, missiles), proxy wars
(Korea, Vietnam, Afghanistan), and the space race. The doctrine of Mutually
Assured Destruction (MAD) deterred direct conflict. Key events include the
Berlin Blockade (1948–49), the Korean War (1950–53), the Cuban Missile Crisis
(1962), the Vietnam War (1955–75), and the Soviet-Afghan War (1979–89). The
Cold War ended with the dissolution of the Soviet Union on December 25, 1991.
It reshaped international alliances, produced decades of proxy conflicts, and
left a lasting imprint on global politics.

[DOC-014] The Silk Road
The Silk Road was a network of trade routes connecting China to the Mediterranean
world from roughly the 2nd century BCE to the 15th century CE. Named after the
lucrative Chinese silk trade, the routes facilitated exchange of goods (spices,
textiles, glassware, precious metals), technologies (papermaking, printing,
gunpowder), religions (Buddhism, Islam, Christianity), and diseases (notably
the Black Death). The overland routes passed through Central Asia, Persia, and
Anatolia; maritime routes linked India, Arabia, and East Africa. The Mongol
Empire's control of Central Asia in the 13th–14th centuries temporarily
stabilized and expanded Silk Road trade. The rise of European maritime trade
routes in the 15th century gradually diminished the overland routes' importance.

[DOC-015] Supply and Demand
The law of demand states that, all else equal, as the price of a good rises,
the quantity demanded falls. The law of supply states that as price rises,
quantity supplied increases. Market equilibrium occurs where supply and demand
curves intersect, determining the equilibrium price and quantity. Price
elasticity of demand measures how responsive quantity demanded is to price
changes. Elastic demand (elasticity > 1) means demand changes significantly
with price; inelastic demand (elasticity < 1) means demand is relatively
unresponsive. Factors shifting demand curves include income, prices of
substitutes and complements, consumer preferences, and expectations. Factors
shifting supply curves include input costs, technology, and the number of
producers.

[DOC-016] Inflation and Monetary Policy
Inflation is the rate at which the general price level of goods and services
rises, eroding purchasing power. It is measured by indices such as the Consumer
Price Index (CPI) and Producer Price Index (PPI). Demand-pull inflation occurs
when aggregate demand exceeds supply. Cost-push inflation results from rising
production costs. Central banks use monetary policy to control inflation:
raising interest rates reduces borrowing and spending, cooling demand; lowering
rates stimulates activity. Quantitative easing (QE) involves central banks
purchasing assets to inject liquidity into the financial system. The US Federal
Reserve, European Central Bank, and other central banks typically target
inflation rates around 2% per year as consistent with stable growth.

[DOC-017] Cryptocurrency and Blockchain
A blockchain is a distributed ledger in which transactions are recorded in
blocks linked cryptographically in a chain. Once recorded, data is extremely
difficult to alter, providing tamper-resistance. Consensus mechanisms—Proof of
Work (PoW) and Proof of Stake (PoS)—allow network participants to agree on the
valid state of the ledger without a central authority. Bitcoin, introduced in
2009 by the pseudonymous Satoshi Nakamoto, was the first decentralized
cryptocurrency. Ethereum extended the concept with smart contracts—self-executing
code on the blockchain that enables decentralized applications (dApps) and
DeFi (decentralized finance). Volatility, regulatory uncertainty, energy
consumption (PoW), and scalability remain key challenges for mainstream adoption.

[DOC-018] The Hard Problem of Consciousness
Philosopher David Chalmers coined the term "hard problem of consciousness" to
describe the difficulty of explaining why and how physical processes in the brain
give rise to subjective experience (qualia). The "easy problems" of consciousness
(explaining attention, memory, behavioral control) are challenging but tractable
through neuroscience and cognitive science. The hard problem asks why there is
"something it is like" to be conscious—why neural activity feels like anything at
all. Physicalist theories argue consciousness is fully reducible to brain
processes. Dualist theories posit non-physical mental substances. Panpsychism
suggests consciousness is a fundamental feature of all matter. Integrated
Information Theory (IIT) proposes that consciousness corresponds to integrated
information (phi) in a system.

[DOC-019] Cognitive Biases
Cognitive biases are systematic patterns of deviation from rationality in
judgment. The availability heuristic leads people to overestimate the likelihood
of events that come easily to mind. Confirmation bias causes individuals to
favor information that confirms preexisting beliefs. The Dunning-Kruger effect
describes the tendency of people with limited knowledge to overestimate their
competence. Anchoring bias occurs when people rely too heavily on the first
piece of information encountered. Loss aversion (from prospect theory by
Kahneman and Tversky) shows that losses are psychologically weighted more
heavily than equivalent gains. Understanding cognitive biases is critical for
improving decision-making in fields from finance and medicine to public policy
and AI system design.

[DOC-020] Operating System Fundamentals
An operating system (OS) is system software that manages hardware resources and
provides services for application programs. Core components include the kernel
(managing CPU, memory, and I/O), process scheduler (allocating CPU time),
memory manager (virtual memory, paging), file system (organizing persistent
storage), and device drivers. Process isolation and virtual memory allow multiple
programs to run simultaneously without interfering. The OS exposes system calls
as an interface between user applications and hardware. Popular general-purpose
OSes include Linux (kernel by Linus Torvalds, 1991), Windows (Microsoft), and
macOS (Apple). Linux dominates server and cloud infrastructure; Windows leads
desktop; Android (Linux-based) leads mobile.

[DOC-021] Computer Networking — TCP/IP
The TCP/IP model is the conceptual framework underlying internet communication.
It has four layers: Link (physical transmission), Internet (IP addressing and
routing), Transport (TCP/UDP), and Application (HTTP, DNS, SMTP). IP addresses
uniquely identify devices on a network; IPv4 uses 32-bit addresses (e.g.,
192.168.1.1), while IPv6 uses 128-bit addresses to accommodate the exhaustion
of IPv4 space. TCP (Transmission Control Protocol) provides reliable, ordered,
error-checked delivery via a three-way handshake and acknowledgment system.
UDP (User Datagram Protocol) is connectionless, faster, but unreliable—suitable
for real-time applications like video streaming and gaming. DNS (Domain Name
System) translates human-readable domain names to IP addresses.

[DOC-022] Cybersecurity Fundamentals
The CIA triad—Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability—is the foundational
model for information security. Encryption protects confidentiality: symmetric
encryption (AES) uses a single key; asymmetric encryption (RSA, ECC) uses
public-private key pairs. TLS (Transport Layer Security) secures data in transit.
Common attack vectors include phishing, SQL injection, cross-site scripting (XSS),
man-in-the-middle (MITM), and ransomware. Defense strategies include firewalls,
intrusion detection systems (IDS), multi-factor authentication (MFA), patch
management, and zero-trust architecture. The NIST Cybersecurity Framework
(Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover) provides a structured approach
to managing cybersecurity risk.

[DOC-023] Narrative Structure
Narrative structure refers to the framework that organizes a story. The
three-act structure divides a story into Setup, Confrontation, and Resolution.
Freytag's Pyramid describes five stages: exposition, rising action, climax,
falling action, and denouement. The Hero's Journey (Joseph Campbell's monomyth)
identifies 17 stages across departure, initiation, and return. Point of view
(POV) choices—first-person, second-person, third-person limited, or omniscient—
shape how readers access characters' inner lives. Unreliable narrators create
tension between stated and implied meaning. Foreshadowing plants clues about
future events; in medias res opens the story in the middle of action. These
structural tools are used across novels, films, games, and interactive media.

[DOC-024] Language Families and Linguistics
There are approximately 7,000 living languages in the world, grouped into
roughly 150 language families. The Indo-European family is the largest by number
of speakers, encompassing Germanic (English, German), Romance (Spanish, French,
Portuguese), Slavic (Russian, Polish), Indic (Hindi, Bengali), and others.
Sino-Tibetan (Mandarin, Cantonese, Tibetan) is the second-largest family by
speakers. The Austronesian family is the most geographically widespread.
Linguistic typology classifies languages by features: SOV (Subject-Object-Verb)
is the most common word order (e.g., Japanese, Turkish); SVO is second (e.g.,
English, Mandarin). Language endangerment affects over 40% of languages, many
with fewer than 1,000 speakers remaining.

[DOC-025] The Life Cycle of Stars
Stars form in molecular clouds of gas and dust when gravity causes regions to
collapse. A protostar forms, and nuclear fusion ignites when core temperature
and pressure are sufficient (about 10 million Kelvin for hydrogen fusion). A
main-sequence star fuses hydrogen into helium in its core. The Sun is a G-type
main-sequence star with an estimated remaining lifespan of ~5 billion years.
When hydrogen is exhausted, a star expands into a red giant. Low-mass stars
shed their outer layers, forming a planetary nebula and leaving a white dwarf.
High-mass stars undergo supernova explosions, leaving neutron stars or, if
massive enough, black holes. The Hertzsprung-Russell (H-R) diagram plots stellar
luminosity against temperature, revealing these evolutionary stages.

[DOC-026] Exoplanet Detection Methods
Over 5,600 exoplanets have been confirmed as of 2024. The transit method detects
planets by measuring the dip in a star's brightness as a planet passes in front.
The radial velocity (Doppler spectroscopy) method detects the wobble a planet
induces in its host star. Direct imaging captures light from the planet itself,
possible only for large planets far from bright stars. Gravitational microlensing
occurs when a planet's gravity bends and amplifies light from a background star.
The Kepler Space Telescope (2009–2018) confirmed over 2,600 exoplanets using
the transit method. The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), launched in 2021,
enables detailed atmospheric characterization of exoplanets through transmission
spectroscopy.

[DOC-027] Macronutrients
The three macronutrients are carbohydrates, proteins, and fats.
Carbohydrates (4 kcal/g) are the body's preferred energy source; complex carbs
(starches, fiber) are digested slowly, while simple sugars cause rapid blood
glucose spikes. Proteins (4 kcal/g) are composed of amino acids; 9 essential
amino acids cannot be synthesized by the body and must come from diet. Complete
proteins (meat, eggs, soy) contain all essential amino acids; incomplete proteins
lack one or more. Fats (9 kcal/g) are essential for hormone production, fat-
soluble vitamin absorption (A, D, E, K), and cell membrane integrity. Saturated
fats (solid at room temperature) are linked to cardiovascular risk when consumed
in excess; unsaturated fats (monounsaturated, polyunsaturated) are generally
considered healthier. Trans fats are strongly associated with cardiovascular disease.

[DOC-028] The Gut Microbiome
The gut microbiome consists of trillions of microorganisms—bacteria, archaea,
fungi, and viruses—residing primarily in the large intestine. The gut microbiome
influences digestion, immune function, metabolism, and even mental health via
the gut-brain axis. Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes are the dominant bacterial
phyla. A diverse microbiome is generally associated with better health outcomes.
Antibiotics can significantly disrupt microbiome composition, and recovery may
take months. Probiotics (live beneficial bacteria, e.g., Lactobacillus,
Bifidobacterium) and prebiotics (dietary fiber that feeds beneficial bacteria)
support microbiome health. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) has shown
efficacy in treating recurrent Clostridioides difficile infections and is
under investigation for other conditions.
